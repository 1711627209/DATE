94 •  2022 IEEE International Solid-State Circuits ConferenceISSCC 2022 / SESSION 5 / IMAGERS, RANGE SENSORS AND DISPLAYS / 5.15.1      A 0.37W 143dB-Dynamic-Range 1Mpixel Backside-            Illuminated Charge-Focusing SPAD Image Sensor with Pixel-            Wise Exposure Control and Adaptive Clocked Recharging  Yasuharu Ota, Kazuhiro Morimoto, Tomoya Sasago, Mahito Shinohara, Yukihiro Kuroda, Wataru Endo, Yu Maehashi, Shintaro Maekawa,  Hiroyuki Tsuchiya, Aymantarek Abdelghafar, Shingo Hikosaka,  Masanao Motoyama, Kenzo Tojima, Kosei Uehira, Junji Iwata,  Fumihiro Inui, Yasushi Matsuno, Katsuhito Sakurai, Takeshi Ichikawa  Canon, Kanagawa, Japan  Demands for single-photon-sensitive high-dynamic-range (HDR) imaging in security, automotive, and medical applications have driven development of scalable single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD)-based image sensors. In recent years, 3D-stacking technology combined with advanced CMOS processes has enabled pixel-parallel photon counting in sub-10μm SPAD pixels. A major technical challenge in realizing high-deﬁnition SPAD image sensors lies in a trade-off between power consumption and dynamic range (DR). SPAD pixels inherently consume a considerable amount of power due to the high-voltage operation and high current gain. Power consumption from the SPAD array (PSPAD) grows signiﬁcantly with increasing incident photon ﬂux, and often dominates over that from the readout circuit under high light conditions. Restricting maximum photon counts per frame could suppress the maximum PSPAD, at the expense of DR. To address this issue, a recharging circuit architecture must be carefully considered. Passive recharging has been widely employed for HDR imaging SPADs [1-3], but it is not a viable option for megapixel implementation due to the huge PSPAD, typically reaching tens of watts at excess illuminance. A clocked recharging architecture provides a scalable solution thanks to its compact circuitry and greatly reduced PSPAD at excess illuminance [4-6], but to date no existing SPAD sensor has simultaneously achieved megapixel resolution, sub-watt total power consumption, and >120dB DR.  In this paper, we present a 3D backside-illuminated (BSI) megapixel charge-focusing SPAD image sensor based on pixel-wise exposure control (PWEC) and adaptive clocked recharging, featuring <0.4W total power consumption, 143dB DR, and seamless global shutter (SL-GS) operation. The sensor block diagram is shown in Fig. 5.1.1. The top tier comprises a 960×960 SPAD array with a pitch of 9.585μm, and each SPAD is electrically connected to the corresponding pixel circuit in the bottom tier via pixel-level bonding. Exposure, recharging, and signal readout for the pixel array are controlled by row and column controllers. A programmable frequency for the global recharge clock ensures adaptive control of clocked recharging. A 14b pixel signal is stored in an in-pixel memory, and then sequentially read to a digital front-end via a readout block. To maximize photon detection efﬁciency (PDE) while minimizing dark count rate (DCR), a charge-focusing SPAD device is used [7]. This device achieves an exceptionally small charge per avalanche event of 12fC, with minimal junction capacitance, contributing to the reduction of PSPAD by a factor of 10× with respect to conventional SPAD devices. The SL-GS operation with up to 90fps enables shutter-distortion-free exposure with nearly 100% temporal aperture and virtually zero parasitic light sensitivity.  Figure 5.1.2 shows a pixel circuit schematic and the corresponding timing diagram to perform PWEC. The pixel circuit comprises a recharge transistor, an 11b digital counter, an exposure controller, a 14b memory for SL-GS, and readout buffers. A reset (RES) signal initializes the state of the pixel. For clocked recharging, CLKB is fed to the recharge transistor when STOP is low, which regulates the number of photon counts per frame. The digital counter counts the photon-induced pulses and disables recharging when the count reaches 2,047. During a full exposure time T deﬁned by EN, the exposure controller receives trigger pulses T0, T1, and T2 to check if the count exceeds a predetermined threshold Nsat/m (Nsat=2,048), where m can be conﬁgured to 2, 4, or 8 by MUX. If the count is below the threshold, the pixel continues to be exposed towards the next trigger pulse. If the count exceeds the threshold, STOP turns to high and disables the recharging, and the counter holds the value. To maximize DR, effective exposure time Teff is set to either T/m3, T/m2, or T/m, with the number of recharge clocks set to 2,048 for the shortest exposure time T/m3. Based on this architecture, each pixel can choose an optimum exposure time from T/m3, T/m2, T/m, and T to avoid saturation, and records the selected exposure time as a 3b temporal code TC<2:0>, which is utilized for recovering the light intensity from the 11b raw photon counts.  A major issue in a previously reported PWEC technique [3], apart from the aforementioned drawback of passive recharging, is that it relies only on a temporal code (TC) to recover intensity for high light conditions. To mitigate quantization noise for the intensity recovery, 14b reference TC lines have to be globally distributed to all the pixels, thereby limiting the scalability. Our approach, in contrast, relies on both 11b photon counts and 3b TC for the intensity recovery; the pixel requires only 3b global lines to deﬁne TC, leading to greatly simpliﬁed implementation of the large-scale arrays. Figure 5.1.3 shows measured median photon counts and PSPAD as a function of incident photon ﬂux for three operation modes: passive recharge, clocked recharge, and clocked recharge with PWEC. Light is uniformly illuminated to a monochrome SPAD array, operating at 30fps. For the passive recharge, photon counts saturate at 2,047, and then drop at high light due to the “paralyzing effect” of SPADs. PSPAD similarly saturates, then slightly decreases due to instantaneous voltage drop, and then jumps beyond 3W when the average interval between incident photons becomes shorter than the dead time of the SPAD. The clocked recharge, with the number of recharge clock Nc set to 2,048 per frame to limit the maximum avalanche events, shows slower saturation for both photon counts and PSPAD, and shows only a mild increase in the power consumption at excess illuminance. Combining the clocked recharge with PWEC (m=8) further extends DR by a factor of 512× with even lower power consumption. Both the photon counts and PSPAD sharply drop when the effective exposure time is switched. The resulting DR for the third mode reaches 143dB with total sensor power consumption up to 0.37W (maximum of 0.04W from the SPAD array and 0.33W from the rest of the readout circuits).  Figure 5.1.4 shows the result of HDR imaging with a color SPAD array, based on PWEC operation (m=8, 30fps). On the left side, spatial maps for raw photon counts and the corresponding TC are exhibited. Thanks to the megapixel resolution and the proposed PWEC, the reconstructed HDR image on the right side reproduces ﬁne details of the target scene for both dark and bright areas. A photon ﬂux of over 3 million photons/pixel/frame at the brightest part of the scene (a ﬁlament of the lamp) is measured without saturation. A ﬂowchart for HDR color image reconstruction is shown at the bottom of Fig. 5.1.4.  Figure 5.1.5 shows an example of adaptive clocked recharging. For simplicity, PWEC is disabled for this experiment. Mid- and low-light scenes are captured with the monochrome SPAD array in two modes; full-saturation (FS) mode with Nc=2,048, and low-noise (LN) mode with Nc=100. For the mid-light scene, FS mode captures the target in natural tone, whereas LN mode led to saturation. For a low-light scene, both FS and LN modes capture the images without saturation. A clustered hot pixel in the magniﬁed image for FS mode is caused by a single hot pixel with high DCR inducing light-emission crosstalk to the adjacent pixels. In contrast, LN mode deﬁnes the upper limit of the avalanche event, and the reduced signal counts at the hot pixel signiﬁcantly suppresses the crosstalk to the adjacent pixels, making it easier to correct in the post-processing. The result suggests that adaptive control of recharge clock frequency based on the scene brightness can potentially improve overall image quality.  Figure 5.1.6 shows a performance summary of the chip and a comparison to the state-of-the-art SPAD image sensors. Based on this table, this work demonstrates one of the largest sensor resolutions with 143dB DR and <0.4W total power consumption. Fill factor, PDE and DCR present best-in-class performance thanks to the charge-focusing SPAD device. Micrographs for the top- and bottom-tier chips of the SPAD image sensor are shown in Fig. 5.1.7.
